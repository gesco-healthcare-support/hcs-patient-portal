# Appendix D: Adversarial verification record

> Every standards claim produced by the 14 research agents was re-checked by a second agent with a
> different instruction: open the URL, and default to "does not support the claim" where the page
> does not clearly settle it. Do not give the benefit of the doubt.
>
> This appendix is the record of what that pass caught. It exists so the receiving session can see
> which claims survived scrutiny and which were corrected or dropped, rather than having to take the
> deliverables on trust.
>
> **Aggregate: 290 claims checked across 14 areas.** Six areas came back `material-errors`, eight
> `minor-corrections`, none `all-sound`.
>
> Produced 2026-08-28.

---

## D0. Why this pass existed

The brief was emphatic that uncited or wrongly-cited assertions are worse than visible holes,
because the receiving team cannot evaluate whether a claimed standard is real. A single research
pass produces confident prose regardless of whether the underlying page says what the agent thinks
it says -- the failure mode is not fabrication so much as **citation drift**: a correct fact attached
to the wrong URL, a threshold remembered rather than read, a version number one release stale.

The verification pass was designed to catch exactly that. Its instruction set named the specific
failure modes to hunt for: thresholds the page contradicts, superseded version numbers, requirement
IDs that do not exist, tools claimed maintained that are archived, regulations misdescribed, and
URLs that 404 or redirect somewhere unrelated.

**It worked.** The most serious catches are listed in D1. None of them reached the deliverables.

---

## D1. The catches that mattered

These are the errors that would have materially misled the receiving team had the single-pass
research been published as-is.

### A fabricated quotation of a W3C success criterion

The accessibility research claimed WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.8 "requires that Content does not rely on
cognitive function test to authenticate unless an alternative authentication method is available."
The verifier pulled the normative text and found the actual wording is different. **A fabricated
quote of a standard, presented to a team that cannot check it, is the single most damaging output
this exercise could have produced.** Dropped entirely.

### A wrong reading of California statute

The same pass claimed California Government Code 7405 flows Section 508 / WCAG 2.0 A+AA conformance
down to private contractors. The verifier read the statute: **7405(a) binds only "state governmental
entities"**; 7405(b) requires a contracting entity merely to respond to and resolve accessibility
complaints. The deliverable now says the opposite of what the first pass produced, and flags the
ADA Title III position as genuinely contested rather than settled.

### A rule count and a rule that do not exist

The angular-eslint template accessibility preset was claimed to contain 12 rules including
`no-positive-tabindex`. The verifier checked `packages/eslint-plugin-template/src/configs/accessibility.json`
at tag v20.7.0: **11 rules, and no such rule.** Corrected in the deliverable.

### A tool behaviour that inverts the recommendation

The claim that axe-core covers WCAG 2.2 omitted the sentence that makes it actionable: axe-core's own
rule descriptions state the WCAG 2.2 rules "are disabled by default, until WCAG 2.2 is more widely
adopted and required." **A passing axe scan does not mean WCAG 2.2 conformance unless the tag is
explicitly enabled.** That correction is now in the deliverable.

### A capability claimed for a tool that does not have it

The coverage research claimed coverlet "can emit every format this project needs from a single test
run AND can fail the build on a threshold", citing one page for both halves. The verifier found that
page explicitly contradicts the threshold half twice: **VSTest integration does not support
thresholds.** The recommendation changed accordingly.

### A conclusion that may reverse

The claim that this project exceeds SonarQube Cloud's 50k-LOC private-analysis cap compared a raw
line count against Sonar's cap. The verifier found Sonar's LOC calculation **excludes test code,
excluded files, unsupported languages, comments and blank lines** -- so the raw count overstates it
and the conclusion may reverse. Marked `UNVERIFIED` in the deliverable rather than asserted.

### Query counts stated from memory

CodeQL's C# suite was claimed to hold 64 queries, 54 default, 10 security-extended-only. The verifier
read the page: **55 built-in, 44 default, 11 extended-only**, and the claim's own enumeration omitted
one of the eleven.

### A date, a licence and a cadence, all wrong

ZAP was claimed to have left OWASP on 2023-08-01 under Apache 2.0 with Monday weekly pre-releases.
The cited page says **September 2023**, says nothing about the licence, and the last five weeklies
fell on a Wednesday, Wednesday, Tuesday, Thursday and Tuesday.

### Six claims citing pages that do not carry their numbers

In the ASVS area alone, six claims carried correct numbers sourced from a different page than the one
cited -- including one citing an **eCFR HIPAA endpoint** as the source for assertions about OWASP
ASVS 5.0.0 and NIST SP 800-218, which can support neither.

## D2. The aggregate result

Across 290 individually re-checked claims:

| Verdict | Count | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `yes` -- page carries the claim as stated | 155 | 53% |
| `partially` -- substance right, citation imprecise or incomplete | 124 | 43% |
| `no` -- wrong as stated | 10 | 3% |
| `could-not-check` -- URL unreachable | 1 | <1% |

**Only 53% of claims survived first-pass scrutiny cleanly.** That number is the argument for the
verification pass existing. The 42% `partially` band is mostly citation drift -- a correct fact
attached to the wrong URL, a threshold that lives on a sibling page, an enumeration that omits one
item. Individually minor; collectively it is the difference between a document a team can check and
one they must take on trust. The 11 `no` verdicts were removed from the deliverables entirely.
